# The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two: Some Limits on our Capacity for Processing Information (1956)
**Author**: George A. Miller  
**Affiliation**: Harvard University  
**Journal**: Psychological Review, 63(2), 81–97, 1956.  

---

## Core Contents & Findings

### 1. Absolute Judgments of Unidimensional Stimuli (Channel Capacity)
- Application of Information Theory (Shannon/Wiener; variance as amount of information, covariance as transmitted information).
- Bit of information: Binary decision unit ($H = \log_2 N$).
- Tones and Pitches (Pollack): Channel capacity for absolute pitch judgment asymptotes at ~2.5 bits (about 6 equally likely alternatives).
- Loudness (Garner): Channel capacity is ~2.3 bits (about 5 discriminable categories).
- Taste intensity (Beebe-Center et al.): ~1.9 bits (4 categories).
- Visual position (Hake & Garner): ~3.25 bits (10–15 categories).
- General unidimensional channel capacity: Mean = 2.6 bits, SD = 0.6 bit (corresponding to 6.5 categories, range roughly 3 to 15, centered near 7).

### 2. Multidimensional Stimuli
- Adding dimensions increases total capacity but with decreasing marginal gains:
  - Dot in a square (2D position, Klemmer & Frick): 4.6 bits (24 categories).
  - Pitch + Loudness (Pollack): 3.1 bits (compared to 2.5 + 2.3 = 4.8).
  - 6 acoustic variables (Pollack & Ficks): 7.2 bits (about 150 categories).
  - 8 binary dimensions (Pollack & Ficks): 6.9 bits (120 categories).
- Distinctive features in phonology: 8-10 binary dimensions allow speech perception.

### 3. Subitizing vs. Estimation
- Kaufman, Lord, Reese, Volkmann: Random patterns of dots for 0.2 s. Up to 5 or 6 dots: errorless ("subitizing"); above 7: estimation. Transmits ~4 bits.

### 4. The Span of Immediate Memory (Chunks vs. Bits)
- Critical distinction between Absolute Judgment (limited by Bits) and Immediate Memory (limited by Chunks).
- Immediate memory span is remarkably constant across materials: ~7 items (decimal digits: 7 items = 23 bits; English monosyllables: 5 words = ~50 bits; binary digits: 9 items = 9 bits).
- Hayes (1952) and Pollack (1953) experiments prove that transmitted information is NOT constant; chunk span is constant.

### 5. Recoding
- Chunking: Grouping input into familiar units.
- Sidney Smith (1954) experiment: Recoding binary digits into octal digits (3:1) and base-16/32 allows span to expand from 8 to 40 binary digits.
- Language as the ultimate recoding system.
